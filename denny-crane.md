# CalFresh Hearing — The Denny Crane Page

## The Rule

Federal SNAP law excludes a person who lives with others and pays them for meals from constituting a separate SNAP household.

I am unquestionably a boarder.

I am not asking the judge to find otherwise.

## Why the Rule Exists

When Congress addressed boarders in 1981, it gave two important reasons for the rule.

First, Congress reasoned that people paying room and board already receive meals, so they do not need food stamps to purchase those meals.

Second, Congress was closing a loophole. Someone who ordinarily belonged to a household could potentially characterize himself as a "boarder," claim to pay the household for meals, and seek SNAP separately. Congress did not want household arrangements reorganized on paper to produce additional benefits.

Neither premise neatly resolves my case.

## There Is No Loophole Here

I actually live in a room-and-board residence operated as part of Hernandez Room and Board, which operates approximately 20 such residences in the San Bernardino area.

I pay for room and board. Meals are provided.

I am not taking an ordinary household and relabeling myself a "boarder" in order to obtain separate SNAP benefits.

**I really am a boarder.**

That is not the weakness in my case. It is why the household-manipulation loophole Congress feared is not what is happening here.

The County can prove that I am a boarder. I concede it.

But proving that fact does not answer the disability question.

## What Makes This Case Different

I have medically documented dietary requirements related to disability, and the meals provided through my room-and-board arrangement do not adequately permit me to follow the diet recommended by my physician.

Congress's other premise was that a boarder already receives the meals that SNAP purchasing power would otherwise help provide.

My circumstances raise the question of what happens when those provided meals do not adequately meet a documented disability-related nutritional need.

## Disability Is Not Foreign to SNAP's Household Rules

Title II of the ADA and Section 504 protect people with disabilities in government benefit programs.

Public entities must consider reasonable disability-related modifications unless the modification would change an essential eligibility requirement or fundamentally alter the nature of the program.

Federal SNAP law itself also permits certain people age 60 or older who cannot purchase and prepare food because of disability to be treated separately for household purposes, subject to statutory conditions.

That provision does not itself override the boarder exclusion.

But it demonstrates something important: individualized disability-related treatment within SNAP's household rules is not a concept foreign to the program.

## The Actual Question

The County's ordinary syllogism is straightforward:

**Boarders are excluded. I am a boarder. Therefore I am excluded.**

Under the ordinary rule, I concede the premises.

But that does not answer the question presented by my request for a disability accommodation:

**Can the boarder rule reasonably be modified for one otherwise eligible person whose disability-related nutritional needs cannot adequately be met by the meals provided, without fundamentally altering SNAP?**

## What I Am Asking For

I am not asking to abolish the boarder rule.

I am not claiming that every disabled boarder should receive CalFresh.

I am not asking for reimbursement because a special diet costs more.

I am not claiming that my residence provides no food.

And I am not manipulating my household status to obtain additional benefits.

I am asking for an individualized accommodation: access to CalFresh purchasing power so that I can supplement the meals I receive with food that permits me to follow my medically documented diet.

That request serves SNAP's stated purpose: safeguarding health and improving nutrition by increasing the food-purchasing power of low-income people.

## The Hardest Question

The strongest argument against me remains straightforward: Congress itself put the boarder exclusion into federal law.

The government may therefore argue that making an exception would fundamentally alter an essential eligibility requirement.

That is the difficult legal question, and it should not be minimized.

But repeatedly proving that I am a boarder does not resolve it.

## The Case in Three Sentences

The question for the judge is not whether I am a boarder.

**I am.**

The question is whether disability law requires a reasonable, individualized modification when neither the household-manipulation problem Congress sought to prevent nor its assumption that the provided meals adequately satisfy the need for food assistance fully describes the case before the judge.
