# CalFresh Hearing Argument

## Working Case Theory

This document develops the argument for the CalFresh administrative hearing concerning the application of the SNAP boarder exclusion to a person with a disability who requires a medically appropriate diet.

This is a working argument. Each legal proposition should be supported by verified primary authority before being presented as established law.

---

## 1. SNAP's Fundamental Purpose

Congress expressly stated the purpose of SNAP in the Food and Nutrition Act.

The program exists to safeguard the health and well-being of the nation's population by raising levels of nutrition among low-income households and increasing their food-purchasing power so they can obtain a more nutritious diet.

This purpose matters when determining whether a requested disability accommodation would fundamentally alter SNAP.

The requested accommodation is not contrary to SNAP's nutritional purpose. It is intended to allow access to food necessary to follow a medically recommended diet.

### Authority

Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, 7 U.S.C. § 2011.

---

## 2. The Boarder Exclusion

The SNAP boarder exclusion originates in federal law rather than merely in a California administrative policy.

Current federal law provides:

> "In no event shall any individual or group of individuals constitute a household if they reside in an institution or boarding house, or else live with others and pay compensation to the others for meals."

### Authority

7 U.S.C. § 2012(n)(4).

The federal SNAP regulations implement this statutory restriction.

Therefore, the argument should not incorrectly characterize the boarder exclusion as merely a discretionary CalFresh policy that California may freely disregard.

---

## 3. The Martin Question: Why Does the Boarder Rule Exist?

The important question is not merely what the boarder exclusion says.

The important question is:

**Why did Congress create it?**

The legislative history of the 1981 change provides unusually direct evidence of congressional purpose.

During consideration of S. 1007, the Congressional Record described the boarder provision as closing a loophole.

Congress explained:

> "If persons pay room and board for their meals, they do not need food stamps since they already receive meals."

The discussion also identified a second concern: a member of an otherwise eligible household might declare himself a boarder and apply separately in order to obtain additional benefits.

Therefore, at least two purposes appear in the legislative history:

1. Congress assumed that people paying room and board were already receiving the meals for which SNAP would otherwise provide purchasing power.

2. Congress sought to prevent manipulation of household status to obtain additional benefits.

### The Loophole Congress Intended to Close

Congress's second concern can be stated in practical terms.

Suppose people who ordinarily constitute a single household live and eat together. Without the boarder restriction, one member might attempt to characterize himself as a "boarder" who pays the others for meals and then apply for SNAP separately. That could allow an existing household arrangement to be reorganized on paper in order to obtain additional benefits.

That is not what is occurring here.

The claimant actually resides in a room-and-board residence. He pays for room and board and acknowledges that meals are provided as part of that arrangement.

The claimant is unquestionably a genuine boarder. He is not attempting to convert an ordinary shared household into multiple SNAP households by relabeling himself as a boarder. His boarder status is not being asserted as a device to obtain separate SNAP eligibility.

Indeed, the claimant concedes the very factual proposition the County is likely to emphasize: **he is a boarder.**

That concession matters because the anti-loophole concern identified by Congress is not implicated. There is no disputed household classification being manipulated to obtain additional benefits.

The real dispute begins after boarder status is conceded.

The question is whether federal disability law requires reasonable modification of the consequence ordinarily attached to that status when the meals provided through the genuine room-and-board arrangement do not adequately permit the claimant to meet his documented disability-related nutritional needs.

### Authority

Congressional Record, June 9, 1981, discussion of S. 1007, "Boarders."

This legislative history must be preserved because it provides evidence of the problems Congress intended the boarder exclusion to solve.

---

## 4. Application of the Martin Question

The requested disability accommodation should be compared with the purposes identified above.

The request does not depend upon pretending that no meals are provided through the room-and-board arrangement.

Meals are provided.

Instead, the issue is whether the congressional assumption underlying the exclusion fully applies when a person with a disability has medically documented nutritional requirements that the provided meals do not adequately permit him to meet.

The question becomes:

**Does allowing supplemental CalFresh purchasing power for medically appropriate food defeat the purposes for which Congress enacted the boarder exclusion?**

That question should be considered separately from the simple fact that the individual technically falls within the ordinary boarder definition.

---

## 5. Disability Law Applies to Public Benefits Administration

Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act applies to state and local government programs and services.

A public entity generally must make reasonable modifications to policies, practices, or procedures when necessary to avoid disability discrimination unless the public entity can demonstrate that the modification would fundamentally alter the nature of the service, program, or activity.

### Authority

Americans with Disabilities Act, Title II.

42 U.S.C. § 12132.

28 C.F.R. § 35.130(b)(7).

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act also prohibits disability discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance.

### Authority

Rehabilitation Act of 1973, § 504.

29 U.S.C. § 794.

---

## 6. Eligibility Criteria and Disability

The Title II regulations also address eligibility criteria.

A public entity may not apply eligibility criteria that screen out or tend to screen out individuals with disabilities from fully and equally enjoying a public program unless those criteria can be shown to be necessary for provision of the program.

### Authority

28 C.F.R. § 35.130(b)(8).

This provision should be analyzed together with the reasonable-modification requirement.

The argument is not that every eligibility requirement affecting a person with a disability becomes invalid.

The question is whether the particular requirement is essential and whether a reasonable modification can provide meaningful access without fundamentally altering the program.

---

## 7. SNAP Already Recognizes Disability in Household Rules

Federal SNAP law already recognizes circumstances in which disability affects the ordinary rules governing household composition.

For example, federal law contains a special separate-household provision for certain people age 60 or older who cannot purchase food and prepare meals because of qualifying disability, subject to additional statutory requirements.

### Authority

7 U.S.C. § 2012(n)(3).

This provision does **not**, by itself, override the separate boarder exclusion in § 2012(n)(4).

It is nevertheless relevant to the broader argument because it demonstrates that disability-related departures from ordinary household-composition rules are not inherently foreign to the structure of SNAP.

---

## 8. The Requested Modification

The requested accommodation should be stated narrowly.

The request is **not**:

- abolition of the boarder rule;

- a declaration that all disabled boarders qualify for SNAP;

- reimbursement for the additional cost of a special diet; or

- a claim that no meals are provided by the room-and-board residence.

The requested modification is:

**Allow an otherwise financially eligible person with a disability to receive CalFresh purchasing power to obtain food necessary to supplement the meals provided through room and board when medically documented dietary needs cannot adequately be met by those provided meals.**

The modification is based upon an individualized disability-related nutritional need.

---

## 9. Medical Necessity

The medical evidence is central to the requested accommodation.

Dr. Wolf's letter should be used to establish exactly what dietary pattern is medically recommended.

The evidence should demonstrate that the need is not simply a preference for different food or a request for a few desirable grocery items.

The argument is that the medically appropriate diet is a pattern of nutrition associated with management of the documented medical condition.

The doctor's actual language should be quoted accurately and should not be embellished.

## 10. Distinguish the Special-Diet Medical Deduction

SNAP rules generally do not treat the cost of a special diet as an allowable medical-expense deduction.

That is **not** the relief requested here.

The argument should therefore avoid saying:

> "I should receive additional SNAP because diabetic food costs more."

Instead:

> "The ordinary boarder exclusion prevents access to SNAP purchasing power even though the meals supplied under the boarding arrangement do not permit me to meet my documented disability-related nutritional needs."

This distinction prevents the accommodation argument from being confused with an unavailable special-diet expense deduction.

---

## 11. Meaningful Access — Alexander v. Choate

A central disability-law concept is meaningful access.

In **Alexander v. Choate, 469 U.S. 287 (1985)**, the Supreme Court considered Tennessee's decision to limit Medicaid recipients to 14 days of inpatient hospital coverage per year.

The plaintiffs argued that the limitation had a disproportionate effect on people with disabilities because they were more likely to require longer hospital stays.

The Supreme Court recognized that Section 504 requires people with disabilities to be provided meaningful access to the benefit offered and that reasonable modifications may sometimes be necessary to provide that access.

But the Court also imposed an important limitation.

Section 504 did not require Tennessee to redefine the benefit it had chosen to provide. Tennessee provided the same 14 days of inpatient coverage to disabled and nondisabled Medicaid recipients. Section 504 did not transform that defined benefit into a guarantee that every recipient receive enough hospital care to achieve an equivalent health outcome.

### Distinguishing Choate

The claimant's requested modification is materially different in an important respect.

The claimant does not request a larger CalFresh allotment because of disability.

He does not contend that, for example, if an otherwise comparable nondisabled household receives a particular monthly allotment, his disability entitles him to a larger allotment in order to achieve an equivalent nutritional outcome.

He requests the same SNAP benefit available to other eligible households, calculated according to the ordinary SNAP benefit formula.

The requested modification therefore concerns **access to the ordinary benefit, not enlargement of the benefit itself.**

This distinction potentially separates the present case from the unsuccessful claim in Choate.

### The Remaining Choate Problem

Choate nevertheless presents an important issue for the claimant.

The County may argue that the statutory boarder exclusion is itself an essential eligibility requirement defining who is entitled to participate in SNAP.

Under that characterization, modifying the boarder exclusion would not merely provide meaningful access to an existing benefit. It would alter the class of people Congress made eligible to receive that benefit.

That argument must be confronted directly.

The claimant's position is that the requested accommodation does not change the amount, nature, or purpose of the SNAP benefit. It requests an individualized disability-related modification of an eligibility barrier so that the claimant may obtain the same benefit, under the same benefit calculation, that the program ordinarily provides.

The ultimate question therefore remains whether modification of the boarder exclusion constitutes a reasonable means of providing meaningful access or instead crosses the line identified in Choate by fundamentally altering the program or redefining the benefit Congress chose to provide.

Choate is consequently important to both sides of this case.

It supports the principle that disability law may require reasonable modifications necessary for meaningful access.

At the same time, it warns that disability law does not require government to redefine the benefit itself or make a fundamental alteration to the program.

---

## 12. Fundamental Alteration

The likely government response is that allowing an independently participating boarder would contradict an express federal SNAP eligibility provision and therefore fundamentally alter the program.

This is the most important legal obstacle to the argument.

The response should focus on the nature and purpose of the requested modification.

The question is:

**Would this individualized disability accommodation actually change the essential nature of SNAP, or would it permit SNAP to accomplish its existing nutritional purpose for a person whose disability makes the ordinary boarder assumption inadequate?**

The government should be required to identify what essential feature of SNAP would actually be altered.

The fact that a modification creates an exception to an ordinary rule does not, standing alone, establish that the modification fundamentally alters the program.

However, because the boarder exclusion appears directly in federal statute, this issue requires particularly careful legal research.

---

## 13. The Congressional Assumption and the Individual Case

The 1981 legislative history is particularly important.

Congress reasoned that boarders who already receive meals do not need food stamps for those meals.

The individual case raises a different factual situation:

- meals are provided;

- the existence of those meals is not disputed;

- the person has medically documented dietary requirements;

- the provided meals may not adequately permit compliance with those medical recommendations; and

- supplemental food purchasing power is requested to address that disability-related gap.

Therefore, the accommodation argument can be framed as addressing a circumstance in which the factual assumption underlying the congressional rule does not completely hold.

This does not automatically establish a legal right to the modification.

It does, however, provide an important answer to the Martin question.

---

## 14. The Benefit Amount Should Be Determined Under the Ordinary SNAP Formula

The claimant does not request that the Administrative Law Judge determine how many dollars are necessary to compensate for his disability-related dietary needs.

Such an approach would create a special benefit amount without a statutory calculation method.

The requested modification concerns access to SNAP.

If that modification is required, the claimant's allotment should then be determined under the same ordinary benefit-calculation rules applicable to other participating households.

The claimant seeks neither an enhanced allotment because of disability nor an arbitrarily reduced allotment because his eligibility arises through a disability-related modification.

This distinction is consistent with the reasoning of **Alexander v. Choate**. The claimant does not seek a greater benefit in order to guarantee a particular nutritional outcome. He seeks meaningful access to the same defined benefit available to other eligible participants.

Accordingly, an arbitrary token allotment — for example, an amount selected merely because it appears sufficient to supplement some of the meals provided by the residence — would not answer the legal question presented.

If disability law requires reasonable modification of the eligibility barrier, the appropriate consequence is access to the ordinary SNAP program, with the benefit determined according to the ordinary SNAP calculation.

The accommodation should modify the barrier to access, not create a different benefit.

---

## 15. Anticipated County Arguments

### Argument: The claimant is a boarder.

Response:

The existence of the boarding arrangement is not necessarily the principal disputed fact. The disability argument assumes for purposes of analysis that the ordinary boarder rule applies and asks whether reasonable modification is nevertheless required.

### Argument: Congress expressly excluded boarders.

Response:

Correct. That makes the fundamental-alteration question important. It does not eliminate the independent obligation to consider applicable federal disability law.

### Argument: SNAP does not pay for special diets.

Response:

The requested relief is not a special-diet medical-expense deduction. It is a request for disability-related modification of an eligibility rule that otherwise eliminates access to SNAP purchasing power.

### Argument: Meals are already provided.

Response:

That was one of the assumptions expressly identified when Congress enacted the boarder exclusion. The disability evidence is relevant precisely because it may demonstrate that the provided meals do not adequately meet the medically documented nutritional needs involved in this individual case.

### Argument: An exception would fundamentally alter SNAP.

Response:

The agency should identify specifically how this narrowly tailored accommodation would alter the essential nature of SNAP, particularly given SNAP's statutory nutritional purpose and existing recognition of disability in portions of its household rules.

## 18. Research Still Required

Before the hearing, research should continue on the following central question:

**Can Title II of the ADA or Section 504 require a reasonable modification of an eligibility restriction contained directly in the federal statute governing a public-benefit program, and under what circumstances would such a modification constitute a fundamental alteration?**

Particular attention should be given to:

- Supreme Court authority;

- Ninth Circuit authority;

- federal public-benefit cases;

- SNAP or other USDA disability cases;

- cases distinguishing essential eligibility requirements from modifiable policies;

- cases applying the fundamental-alteration defense;

- Section 504 cases involving federally funded benefit programs;

- legislative history of the 1981 boarder exclusion;

- USDA interpretations of the boarder provisions; and

- California authority concerning reasonable accommodations in CalFresh administration.

No case should be included as supporting authority until the actual opinion has been located and verified.

## 19. Citation Verification Rule

Because inaccurate or fabricated legal citations can seriously damage credibility, every authority used in the final hearing submission must be independently verified.

For every case:

1. Confirm that the case exists.

2. Open the actual judicial opinion from a reliable source.

3. Confirm the court, year, and citation.

4. Confirm that the quoted or paraphrased proposition actually appears in the opinion.

5. Determine whether later authority has limited, distinguished, or overruled the relevant holding.

For every statute or regulation:

1. Verify the current text from an official government source whenever possible.

2. Confirm the subsection.

3. Determine whether the provision was in effect at the relevant time.

For legislative history:

1. Locate the actual committee report, Congressional Record, or other primary legislative document.

2. Preserve the page containing the relevant language.

3. Quote only language actually appearing in the source.

**No citation becomes a brick in the hearing argument merely because an AI generated it.**

## 20. Current Core Theory

The present theory can be summarized as follows:

**Congress excluded boarders from SNAP partly on the assumption that people paying room and board already receive the meals that SNAP purchasing power would otherwise help them obtain. The claimant does receive meals and does not dispute that fact. However, because of disability-related medical needs documented by his physician, those meals may not provide meaningful access to the medically appropriate nutrition he requires. Title II of the ADA and Section 504 require consideration of reasonable disability-related modifications, subject to the essential-eligibility and fundamental-alteration limitations. The claimant therefore requests a narrowly tailored modification allowing access to CalFresh purchasing power to supplement the provided meals with medically appropriate food. The central legal question is whether that individualized modification can coexist with the essential purposes and requirements of SNAP or would fundamentally alter the program.**

That question should be resolved from verified statutory text, legislative history, disability law, case law, medical evidence, and the county's eventual Position Statement—not from assumptions about either side's position.
